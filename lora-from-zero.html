A field guide · frozen + trainable · no prior fine-tuning knowledge required

LoRA, from zero.

How to teach a model with billions of knobs a new skill — while touching almost none of them. One idea, six short steps, and one slider you get to play with.

frozen — never changes trainable — the part you teach
Step 1 · the problem

A model is a mountain of dials

Strip away the mystique and a language model is a huge collection of numbers called weights (or parameters). They live in grids called matrices. When text flows through the model, it gets multiplied through these grids, layer after layer, and the values of the weights determine what comes out. A "7B" model has ~7 billion of these numbers.

Fine-tuning means adjusting those numbers so the model gets better at something specific — your company's support style, medical text, SQL, whatever. The classic way, full fine-tuning, nudges every single weight. That works, but look at what it costs:

The analogy to keep in your pocket

The base model is a printed encyclopedia. Full fine-tuning is reprinting the entire thing to change how it discusses one topic. LoRA is adding sticky notes to the relevant pages: the print stays untouched, the notes are cheap to write, and you can peel them off or swap in a different set anytime.

Step 2 · the insight

The change is simpler than the model

Here's the observation that makes LoRA work. When you fine-tune, you're not rebuilding the model's knowledge of language — that's already there. You're making a focused edit: a shift in tone, a new format, a domain vocabulary. The researchers behind LoRA (Hu et al., Microsoft, 2021) noticed that this weight change tends to be low-rank.

"Rank" sounds scary, but the intuition is friendly: a matrix's rank measures how many independent patterns it actually contains. A giant grid of numbers can secretly be very simple — built by repeating and blending just a few basic patterns.

Why this is plausible

Think about what "learn to answer in our support tone" really changes: not the model's grammar, not its world knowledge — a handful of consistent, repeated adjustments. A few patterns, applied broadly. That is exactly what a low-rank matrix is good at expressing.

Step 3 · the mechanic

Freeze W. Learn B × A.

So instead of editing the original weight matrix W, LoRA writes the fine-tune as an update on the side: ΔW ("delta-W", the change). And because we believe the change is low-rank, we don't store ΔW as a full matrix — we factor it into two skinny ones:

Wfrozen  +  ΔW   where   ΔW = B × A Output = the frozen model's answer + a small learned correction. Only B and A ever train.

Two small details that make it "just work"

Day one is a no-op. B starts as all zeros, so at the first training step B×A = 0 and the model behaves exactly like the original. Training then grows the correction from nothing. No risk of scrambling the model before learning begins.

A scaling dial. The correction is actually applied as (α / r) · B×A, where α (alpha) is a constant you pick. Think of it as the volume knob on the adapter — how loudly the sticky notes speak over the original print. A common recipe is α = r or α = 2r.

At rank 8 on a 4096-wide layer, you train 0.39% of the parameters a full fine-tune would touch — and in practice, that's often enough.

Now multiply that saving across every layer of the model. In the original paper, applying LoRA to GPT-3 175B cut trainable parameters by roughly 10,000× and GPU memory during training by about 3×, with quality on par with full fine-tuning on their benchmarks. The finished adapter file is megabytes, not the gigabytes of a full model copy.

Step 5 · where it attaches

Where the strips actually go

A transformer isn't one matrix — each layer has several: the attention projections (usually called Q, K, V, O) and the feed-forward (MLP) matrices. LoRA attaches a B×A pair alongside whichever ones you choose — these choices are your target modules.

Step 6 · after training

Merge it — or collect adapters like cartridges

When training ends you hold two things: the untouched base model, and a tiny adapter. You now have two great options.

Option 1 · Merge for zero-cost inference

Because B×A has the same shape as W, you can literally add them once — W′ = W + (α/r)·B·A — and ship a single ordinary matrix. The deployed model runs at exactly the same speed as the original: no extra layers, no added latency. This is a key advantage over older adapter methods that inserted new layers into the model.

Option 2 · Keep them separate and swap

Bonus round · QLoRA, in one minute

If the frozen base never trains, why store it in full precision? QLoRA (2023) squeezes the frozen weights down to 4-bit numbers — roughly a quarter of the memory — while the small adapters stay in higher precision and train as usual. Result: fine-tuning a 65B-parameter model became possible on a single 48 GB GPU. Same LoRA idea, cheaper freezer.

Step 7 · the knobs

The settings you'll actually meet

Open any fine-tuning library (like Hugging Face's peft) and you'll see a handful of LoRA settings. You now know what every one of them means:

KnobWhat it isRule of thumb
rThe rank — width of the B and A strips. Capacity of the edit.Start at 8–16. Raise it (32–64) for harder, broader tasks; more isn't always better.
lora_alphaThe volume knob — the update is scaled by α / r.Common recipes: α = r, or α = 2r. Change one, mind the other.
target_modulesWhich matrices get adapters (Q, V, all linear layers…)."All linear layers" is the modern safe default.
lora_dropoutRandomly mutes bits of the adapter during training to prevent memorizing.0–0.1. Small datasets → nudge it up.
learning rateStep size for training B and A.Usually ~10× higher than full fine-tuning (≈1e-4 vs 1e-5), since so few weights are learning.

Honest limits, too: LoRA shines at teaching style, format, and domain behavior. It's weaker at cramming in large amounts of brand-new factual knowledge — a low-rank edit can only bend the model so far. For that, people reach for retrieval (RAG), bigger ranks, or full fine-tuning.

The whole guide in six lines

  1. Models are huge grids of numbers (weights); fine-tuning classically edits all of them — expensive.
  2. The change a fine-tune makes is usually simple: low-rank.
  3. So: freeze W ❄, and learn the change as two skinny strips, ΔW = B × A 🔥.
  4. Trainable parameters drop to <1%; B starts at zero so day one is harmless; α/r sets the volume.
  5. After training, merge for zero extra latency — or keep adapters as swappable ~MB "cartridges".
  6. QLoRA = same trick with the frozen base squeezed to 4-bit, so it fits on one GPU.
